# Section 2 Incident Response Procedure (Third-Party Minor Complaints)
**Version:** 1.0
**Status:** Active operational procedure. Companion document to the Parent/Guardian Media & Consent Agreement (`consent-agreement-TEMPLATE.md`) Section 2. Addresses Gap #4 from the Track A hypothetical run: "No complaint response procedure exists for third-party-minor incidents."

**What this is:** Section 2 of the consent agreement establishes the RULE (default to private delivery for footage with identifiable third-party minors; public posting requires specific conditions). This document establishes what to do when the rule wasn't enough — when a complaint comes in about content that's already public.

---

## Why this is needed

Event coverage involves judgment calls made in real time by a creator (often a teenager) and/or their parent. Even with Section 2's clear default-to-private rule, a mistake or disagreement will eventually happen: a wide shot that seemed fine turns out to clearly identify someone else's kid, or a parent from another team objects to something that was actually handled correctly per Section 2 but they're uncomfortable with it anyway. Both cases need a calm, consistent response — not an improvised one each time.

## The procedure

**Step 1 — Acknowledge quickly, without arguing.** Whoever receives the complaint (the creator, the parent, or Subject Medias if it comes through a company channel) responds within 24 hours: "Thanks for flagging this — we take this seriously and will look into it right away." No defensiveness, no immediate explanation of why the post was fine, even if it probably was.

**Step 2 — Pull the content within 24 hours of the complaint, by default.** Unless there's a clear and immediate reason not to (e.g., the complaint is obviously made in bad faith or about a completely different person), the content comes down first, discussion happens after. This is a default, not a debate — the cost of pulling content that was actually fine is low; the cost of leaving up content that's actually a real privacy problem while you deliberate is not.

**Step 3 — Document the complaint.** Who complained, what content, what they said, when. This isn't about building a legal case — it's about having an honest record so patterns can be noticed (e.g., if the same type of shot keeps generating complaints, that's a signal Section 2's guidance needs sharpening).

**Step 4 — Review the original decision against Section 2's standard.** Was the content actually a wide/action shot with no other individual clearly identifiable (Section 2.2's first bullet)? Was there an organizer blanket-release in place (second bullet)? Had the specific family said yes (third bullet)? If the original decision was consistent with Section 2, that's useful to know — it means the RULE may need adjustment, not that anyone did anything wrong. If the original decision was NOT consistent with Section 2 (i.e., a mistake was made), that's also useful to know, and worth a direct, honest conversation with the creator/parent about what happened.

**Step 5 — Respond to the complaining family.** Once content is down and the situation is understood: "We've removed the content and taken a look at what happened. [Brief, honest explanation appropriate to what was actually found in Step 4]." Don't over-explain or get defensive. A short, honest, action-oriented response closes most complaints without escalation.

**Step 6 — If the review reveals Section 2's guidance needs sharpening, that's a real finding.** Note it, and consider whether the consent agreement itself needs a future revision (the same way Section 5 got added when a real gap was found). Incident response isn't just damage control — it's a feedback loop into the actual rule.

## What this procedure does NOT do

- Does not promise the family anything about legal liability, compensation, or formal resolution beyond removing the content and explaining what happened.
- Does not require involving an attorney for routine complaints — reserve that for situations that escalate beyond a normal good-faith disagreement about a photo.
- Does not change Section 2's underlying rule — this procedure handles what happens when the rule wasn't sufficient in a specific instance; rule changes are a separate, deliberate decision (see Step 6).
