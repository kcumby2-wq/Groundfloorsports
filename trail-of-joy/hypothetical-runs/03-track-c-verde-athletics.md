# Hypothetical Full Run — Track C — "Verde Athletics" (fictional client)
**Purpose:** End-to-end rehearsal of a Track C engagement (outside business client paying for AI creative services, where the client's customers/athletes — minors — are the creative subjects). The creative workflow agents already had extensive practice runs on the Capo case; this hypothetical focuses on the parts the agent runs didn't cover: the relationship and contract phases before the creative agents run, and the operational reality after content goes live.

**Assumption convention:** Same as prior runs — `[ASSUMED]` flags judgment calls, `[GAP]` flags system spec gaps.

**The fictional client:** Verde Athletics — a small youth soccer / futsal training program in The Colony, TX (just north of Plano). Owner-operated by Coach Elena Verde, former college player. 14 months in. Roster of ~30 athletes, ages 10-16, mostly mid-level competitive youth soccer. Elena wants to upgrade her social presence and is considering AI creative services. She found Subject Medias through a recommendation from another local coach.

Deliberately *not* Capo, even though Capo was the practice case. Verde Athletics is a slightly different beast — soccer not football, younger athlete cohort (some 10-12 year olds, not just high schoolers), all-female team (so the Soul ID / fabrication concerns get an even harder test).

## Stage 0 — First contact

**Hypothetical scenario:** Elena emails Subject Medias' general inbox. Includes a paragraph about Verde Athletics, asks if Subject Medias does work for small training programs, what it costs, and what AI is involved. Her email reads thoughtfully — she's done some reading.

This is a different entry pattern from both Track A (warm parent DM) and Track B (Groundfloorsports-initiated). **Track C entry tends to be inquiry-driven adult professional contact.** Elena's email is closer to a B2B inquiry than to anything we've seen.

**[GAP #1 (Track C): No documented Track C inquiry-response template / SLA.]** Same shape as Track A's Gap #1 but a different context. A small business owner asking about creative services expects a B2B response cadence (24-48 hours, professional tone, actual answer to her cost question or at least a real "let's get on a call to discuss"). The system has no template for this.

**Action taken:** I'll assume Subject Medias responds within one business day with a brief overview of services, a sentence about the AI involvement (creative agents + human review at every step, no Soul ID training on minor subjects), and a link to schedule a 30-minute discovery call. [ASSUMED: response within 1 business day. Important that this is FASTER than Track A's "few hours via DM" — different context, different reasonable cadence.]

## Stage 1 — Discovery call

**Hypothetical scenario:** 30-minute video call with Elena. The questions Subject Medias needs to answer to enter CBI are:

1. What does Verde Athletics actually do, and what's the brand?
2. Who appears in the content? (Critical — adult coach, or athletes, or both?)
3. Are the athletes minors? (Yes — most are 12-16.)
4. Does Elena have parent-signed releases? In what format? Do they cover AI use?

This is the Track C screening conversation that CBI v1.2's intake formalizes. Elena's answers:

- Verde Athletics: small youth soccer/futsal program, 30 athletes, 10-16, mid-level competitive
- Content subjects: athletes are the primary subjects; Elena herself is occasional
- Yes, all minors
- Releases: she uses a release she got from a coaching certification body. It covers "promotional photos and video." It does NOT explicitly address AI-generated or AI-derivative use.

**This is the critical moment.** Per the Track C policy (`TRACK-C-OUTSIDE-CLIENT-MINOR-SUBJECTS.md`) and the new Track C Service Agreement template (Section 8.1), **a generic release that doesn't address AI/derivative use is insufficient.**

Elena's releases are not Track C-compliant.

**Options at this stage:**

A) Decline the engagement until Elena updates her release language to cover AI-derivative use, has parents re-sign, and demonstrates the updated releases are in place. (Most conservative.)

B) Proceed with the engagement scoped to ONLY content that doesn't feature identifiable minor subjects — environmental b-roll, Coach Elena solo, wide-action shots where no individual minor is clearly identifiable. Athletes-as-subjects content waits until release language is fixed. (Phased approach.)

C) Take a "we'll send our recommended release language, you collect new signatures, when you're done we start" approach. (Practical middle ground.)

**Action taken:** I'll assume (C). Subject Medias provides recommended release language (which would need to be drafted — see Gap #2 below). Elena commits to re-signing all athletes' parents within 3 weeks. Engagement is paused until that's done.

**[GAP #2 (Track C): There's no "recommended AI-derivative release language" template Subject Medias provides to Track C clients.]** The Track C policy says clients need releases that cover AI use. The service agreement template requires it. But there's no template Subject Medias actually hands clients to help them get there. A small business owner like Elena isn't going to draft good language herself; she'll either copy something from the internet (probably bad) or just keep using what she has and hope (worse). 

**Recommendation:** Subject Medias should maintain a "Recommended Parent Release Language for AI/Derivative Use" template that clients can adapt. This is small and high-value — a one-pager with the specific phrasing Section 8.1 requires.

## Stage 2 — The 3-week pause

Nothing operational happens during this gap. Elena is collecting re-signatures. Subject Medias is waiting.

**[GAP #3 (Track C): No "client during paperwork pause" process.]** The system has no record of "Elena is paused while she gets her releases sorted." That's a real lead-management gap — at scale, dozens of inquiries could be in various paused states simultaneously. Some will come back; some won't. The system has no documented way of tracking this. Hubspot or another CRM would normally fill this gap; should be acknowledged that some kind of pipeline-tracking is needed.

**Action taken:** I'll assume Subject Medias has informal tracking (a spreadsheet, an Airtable, even a calendar reminder) and reaches out to Elena at the 3-week mark to check in. [ASSUMED: this happens reliably. In reality, leads in paused states fall through the cracks regularly.]

Three weeks later: Elena has 26 of 30 athletes' updated releases. Four parents haven't responded. She's ready to start with the 26 athletes.

**[GAP #4 (Track C): What happens with the 4 athletes whose parents didn't return signed releases?]** Per the Track C policy, those 4 athletes cannot be subjects of generated content. But they're still on the roster, still at every event, still in every group shot. The system needs a documented "non-cleared subject" handling — wide shots that don't focus on them are fine, individual-subject content featuring them is not, group content that includes them but isn't centered on them is a judgment call.

This is the same shape as Track A's third-party-minor problem but with a sharper edge — these aren't strangers; they're roster athletes. Elena will have ongoing interaction with their families. The handling needs to be careful and consistent.

**Action taken:** Subject Medias and Elena agree: any content featuring the 4 non-cleared athletes is excluded. Group/team shots where they're visible but not the subject are OK if they're not identifiable as individuals. Elena will keep working on getting the remaining 4 releases.

## Stage 3 — Service Agreement signing

**Hypothetical scenario:** With releases confirmed for 26 athletes, Subject Medias and Verde Athletics sign the Track C Client Service Agreement (the template from this session).

Elena reviews the document. Real questions she'd ask:

1. "What does Section 7.2 mean? I'm not sure if I want you reposting our content on Subject Medias' page or not." 

This is the operator-reposting election. Elena is small business; she doesn't know if reposting helps her brand or competes with it. Subject Medias would need to explain the trade-off honestly: reposting on Subject Medias' page gives Verde Athletics exposure to Subject Medias' audience (mostly North Texas youth sports families); the cost is Subject Medias also gets exposure to Verde Athletics' branded content for its own marketing.

[ASSUMED: Elena elects to grant reposting rights in Section 7.2, with attribution.]

2. "What's the fee structure?"

**[GAP #5 (Track C): The Service Agreement template doesn't include the actual fee structure — that's left to a separate scope/pricing document.]** Same shape as Track B's Gap #2 — the system doesn't have pricing answers. A real engagement needs:
- Setup fee (one-time)
- Monthly retainer
- Per-asset pricing (or all-inclusive in the retainer?)
- What's included vs out-of-scope

This is real business decision-making, not documentation work. But the documentation gap matters because Subject Medias can't issue a real Service Agreement without a real fee structure.

**Action taken:** I'll assume a placeholder structure — $2,000 setup + $1,200/month for the agreed scope (8 assets/month + 1 monthly highlight video + monthly sales-deck slides as needed). [ASSUMED: these numbers. As with Track B's licensing fee, I am not recommending these — they're placeholders to keep the run moving.]

3. "When does this start?"

Service Agreement signed, effective immediately. CBI intake call scheduled for the following week.

## Stage 4 — CBI v1.2 intake (the agent run)

We've already done this part extensively in the Capo Track C practice runs. For Verde Athletics, CBI v1.2 would:

- Identify track: C
- Build out the structured subjects list (Coach Elena = adult/client_self; 26 individual athletes = minor + Track C-compliant releases on file; the gym/field = environment)
- Auto-insert the four prohibited operations for minor subjects
- Set per-agent `ready_for` flags
- Output a clean brief

**This works.** The agent architecture proven on Capo applies cleanly to Verde Athletics. No new gaps from the agent itself.

**One real consideration that's specific to Verde and not Capo:** the athletes are younger (some are 10-12, not high school). The voice and aesthetic choices for content featuring 10-year-old soccer players are *different* from those for 16-year-old football players. The brief's `aesthetic_keywords` and `tone_words` need to reflect that, and Elena would need to think carefully about what her brand voice sounds like when the subjects are kids that young.

**[GAP #6 (Track C): No special handling in CBI for "younger minor subjects."]** The prohibited operations cover any minor. But there's no built-in additional sensitivity for very young minors (under ~12). A 10-year-old's content needs even more careful framing than a 15-year-old's. The current system treats all minors as one category; there's an argument for finer-grained handling.

**Recommendation worth considering:** an additional auto-inserted prohibited operation for minor subjects under 13 (or whatever line you draw): no content that frames them as already-competitive athletes vs as kids enjoying a sport. This would prevent "future D1 prospect" framing for a 10-year-old — even more strongly than the existing forward-looking-claims prohibition.

[ASSUMED for this run: Subject Medias and Elena agree to extra-conservative framing for the under-13 cohort without formalizing it in the brief. Real fix would be to formalize.]

## Stage 5 — SLS v1.1 + CCG v1.2 + AGV v1.1 (the agent chain)

These run as designed. Practice work in the Capo runs already validated the chain. For Verde Athletics, no new gaps from the agents themselves — same chain, same enforcement, same outputs.

**One real challenge:** Elena's voice in the existing Verde Athletics IG is *quieter* than Capo's. Her captions are warm, encouraging, family-focused. CCG's voice_lock for Verde would be very different from Capo's blunt work-first voice. **The voice_prompt_fragment needs to land softer.**

Hypothetical voice_prompt_fragment for Verde:

Verde Athletics is a small youth soccer program; the brand voice is Coach Elena's — supportive, never hyped, always honoring the kid's effort over the outcome. This is materially different from Capo's voice. The agent architecture handles it cleanly because the voice fragment is the load-bearing constraint — different clients get different fragments. ✓

## Stage 6 — Content goes live + DSC v1.1 cross-population check

First week of content gets approved by Elena. The 8 assets go to Verde Athletics' IG via Postiz, per Section 6 of the Service Agreement.

**Then Subject Medias asks Elena: "Can we feature one of these on @subjectmedias?"**

DSC v1.1's cross-population check fires. Source population: `track_c_client` (Verde). Destination population: `company` (Subject Medias). The check needs:
- Section 7.2 election (granted by Elena ✓)
- Per-piece Elena approval (need to capture)
- Attribution to @verdeathletics (need to set up)
- The specific piece's minor-subject releases cover use beyond Verde's own marketing — **this is the Section 8.4 check**

Elena's updated release language: I assumed in Stage 1 that Subject Medias provided recommended language. If that language was good, it would explicitly cover Verde's "service providers and creative partners" using the content. But it would NOT automatically cover Subject Medias using the content for *Subject Medias' own marketing*.

**[GAP #7 (Track C): Section 8.4's release-scope check is doing real work, but the system doesn't surface to clients in plain language what that check actually requires.]** Elena, in good faith, may think her updated AI-derivative releases authorize anything. They don't — they authorize Verde's use, plus Subject Medias' use *on Verde's behalf*. Subject Medias using Verde's content for Subject Medias' *own* marketing is a different category that requires either:

A) Even broader release language ("Verde's service providers may use this content for their own marketing with attribution to Verde")
B) Separate per-piece parent confirmation for cross-population reposts

Most clients won't have language broad enough for (A) unless Subject Medias explicitly recommends it. (B) is the realistic fallback.

**Action taken:** For this run, I'll assume the asset in question is the environmental b-roll (the gym, no athletes in frame). That clears Section 8.4 trivially because no minor subject is featured. Elena approves the repost. The piece goes up on @subjectmedias with attribution to @verdeathletics. DSC's audit trail captures everything. ✓

**The harder case** would be a piece featuring an identifiable athlete. For that, per Gap #7, we'd need to halt at DSC's Section 8.4 check, ask Elena to get specific per-piece parent confirmation, and only proceed after that confirmation is on file. The architecture works; the operational reality is friction for everyone involved.

## Stage 7 — The hardest case (six months in)

**Hypothetical scenario:** Six months into the engagement, Subject Medias has produced a substantial library of content for Verde Athletics. Elena's social presence is markedly improved. Verde Athletics' enrollment is up 15%.

A regional youth soccer publication asks Subject Medias to feature Verde Athletics in a "small programs to watch" article. They want to use some of Verde's content (originally produced for Verde's own marketing) in the article. Permission needed from: Verde Athletics. The publication is not Subject Medias' own brand; it's a third-party media outlet.

**This is now a four-way conversation:**
1. The publication wants to feature Verde
2. Subject Medias produced the content for Verde
3. Verde holds the parent releases for the athletes
4. The parents originally signed for Verde's use, then updated for AI-derivative use by Verde and Verde's service providers (per Stage 2's fix)

**Does the publication get to use Verde's content?** Per the existing chain:

- Subject Medias has no rights it can grant the publication; the content was produced for Verde
- Verde would need to give the publication permission
- The parents' release language may or may not cover third-party publication use — depends on how broad the updated language is
- The athletes featured in the specific pieces being used would need to be covered specifically (the 26 cleared athletes, not the 4 unreleased ones)

**[GAP #8 (Track C): No documented process for third-party-media use of Track C content.]** Subject Medias has experienced this complexity for the first time only now; the existing system doesn't address it. This is real-world stuff that will come up at any scale.

**Recommendation:** A documented "third-party-media use" path that establishes (1) Subject Medias is not a gatekeeper to Verde's content for third-party use; (2) Verde is the contact for any third-party requests; (3) Subject Medias can assist with permissioning logistics if Verde asks but doesn't drive the process; (4) the parent-release-scope check still applies to whoever's using the content.

**Action taken:** I'll assume the publication, Verde, and the parents work it out for one piece featuring an athlete whose family is enthusiastic about the press attention. ✓

| 8 | No documented process for third-party-media use of Track C content | Low (but real) | A "third-party media use" handling doc |

**What did NOT break:** Once paperwork was right, the agent chain (CBI → SLS → CCG → AGV → DSC) worked exactly as designed. The Track C policy held — non-compliant releases blocked the engagement; updated releases unblocked it; the cross-population check did real work at Stage 6.

**The biggest finding from Track C:** the system has the right structural pieces but is missing the *client-facing* materials. Subject Medias has internal policies (Track C policy doc), internal agreements (Service Agreement template), and an internal agent chain. What's missing is the stuff Elena actually sees: a recommended release template, a plain-English explanation of Section 8.4, a fee structure that exists. These are real friction points that, if not built, would turn every Track C engagement into a lot of back-and-forth as Subject Medias re-explains the same things every time.

**Compared to Track A and B:** Track C is the most paperwork-heavy of the three at the front end (release language, service agreement, cross-population permissions). But once paperwork is right, the operational reality is *cleanest* — the agent chain handles most of the day-to-day. Track A is the opposite — light paperwork, heavy operational (mentorship, content sorting, ongoing per-post decisions). Track B is the quietest day-to-day but the longest tail of unresolved questions (the licensing fee, the at-majority right, the annual update).
