# Hypothetical Full Run — Track B — "Devon Walker" (fictional athlete)
**Purpose:** End-to-end rehearsal of a Track B engagement (Groundfloorsports-filmed athlete: footage ownership + flat one-time licensing fee model). Different population from Track A — this isn't a creator building a business; this is an athlete whose footage Groundfloorsports owns and may eventually license commercially.

**Assumption convention:** Same as Track A run — `[ASSUMED]` flags judgment calls, `[GAP]` flags system spec gaps.

**The fictional client:** Devon Walker, 16 years old, sophomore safety at a Class 5A high school in Frisco, TX. Plays travel 7v7 in the offseason. His parents are Tracy and Marcus Walker; Tracy handles all of Devon's recruiting / media decisions. Devon is a legitimate recruiting prospect — early conversations with regional D1 programs, ranked ~middle of his state position cohort. Groundfloorsports wants to film him at an upcoming 7v7 event and retain the footage.

---

## Stage 0 — How Track B athletes enter the system at all

This is the first real difference from Track A, and it's a big one.

**Track A starts with a parent DMing Subject Medias because they're interested in onboarding their kid as a creator.** That's a clear, intent-driven entry point.

**Track B starts with Groundfloorsports identifying an athlete it wants to film.** That's a *Groundfloorsports-initiated* contact, not a family-initiated one. The family didn't reach out; we did.

**[GAP #1 (Track B): There's no documented "athlete acquisition" workflow. How does Groundfloorsports identify which athletes to approach? How does first contact get made? Who makes it?]**

This is genuinely a bigger structural question than it looks. For Track B to work at scale, there has to be a real process for:
- Identifying athletes worth filming (recruiting databases, scout networks, in-person scouting at events, parent referrals)
- Making first contact (cold email, cold DM, conversation at an event, introduction through a coach)
- Establishing legitimacy quickly enough that a stranger DM about filming a kid doesn't read as creepy

The last point is real and important. A random message saying "we want to film your son at his tournament next weekend and retain the footage for potential future commercial use" — without context, that lands very badly. The brand needs to precede the ask.

**Action taken:** I'll assume Tracy Walker has heard of Groundfloorsports / Subject Medias because Devon's 7v7 coach mentioned them after seeing them at a previous tournament. So this is a warm-ish lead — not cold, not pre-qualified. [ASSUMED: warm intro from coach. In reality, this is the question of "who does the introducing" that the GAP names.]

## Stage 1 — First conversation with Tracy

**Hypothetical scenario:** Phone call, ~20 minutes. Groundfloorsports rep explains the model: we film Devon at his next event, we own the footage, the Walker family gets a flat one-time licensing fee IF the footage is ever licensed commercially in the future (e.g. a brand wants it for an ad).

Tracy asks the obvious question: "What's the licensing fee?"

**[GAP #2 (Track B): The Groundfloorsports Footage Licensing Agreement template explicitly does not set the dollar amount. The internal notes say it's a real business decision and pricing it too low looks exploitative once a real commercial deal happens.]**

This is a real gap. In the hypothetical, I'd have to either pick a number or refuse to answer. Either way, it exposes that **the fee question isn't answered anywhere in the system** — and it's the first thing any reasonable parent will ask.

I'd recommend this number get set before any Track B contract is ever sent. Not because the number itself is critical, but because being asked "what does this pay" and answering "we haven't figured that out yet" is a credibility problem. Even a range ("between $X and $Y depending on use") would be better than nothing.

**Action taken:** I'll assume Groundfloorsports has decided on a baseline: $500 flat fee per commercial use. [ASSUMED: $500. I am explicitly NOT recommending this number — it's a placeholder, and the real decision is a real business decision that should consider market norms for youth athlete footage licensing.]

Tracy's reaction: "Just $500 if it ends up in a national commercial?" She's not impressed.

This is the second part of Gap #2 — **$500 is probably too low** for the use case the licensing agreement actually contemplates. The Travis Hunter / Bryce Young analogy from earlier in this whole project was about footage of a now-famous athlete being licensed for a real national commercial — that's a significant commercial transaction, and a $500 flat fee to the family looks exploitative when it does. Real pricing thinking is needed.

**Action taken:** I'll flag this and move on. The real conversation with Tracy would be uncomfortable until the pricing is set defensibly.

## Stage 2 — Sending the agreement

The Groundfloorsports Footage Licensing Agreement template gets sent to Tracy.

**She reads it. She has questions:**

1. "What does 'commercial use' actually mean? If you put Devon's footage on your own Instagram, is that commercial?" 

The template addresses this — Section 2 distinguishes commercial licensing (brand ads, broadcast packages) from standard recruiting/social use. Tracy's question is well-asked. Per the template, posting on Groundfloorsports' own Instagram is NOT commercial licensing — that's a brand ownership use, separately covered.

2. "What if Devon decides at 18 he wants the footage to be private?"

The template doesn't fully address this. Section 1 says Groundfloorsports owns the footage. Section 3 only triggers if commercial use happens. But what about Devon at 18 saying "I want the footage destroyed"? The template doesn't say.

**[GAP #3 (Track B): No process for athlete-initiated takedown at majority.]** This is real. A minor athlete grows up to be an adult, and that adult has a right to be heard about what gets done with their own image. The current template gives Groundfloorsports permanent ownership but doesn't establish a Devon-at-18 review point. In contrast, the Track A consent agreement (v1.1, Section 5.5) has explicit revocability for the parent. Track B's template has none for the athlete.

I'd recommend a real fix to the template: an "athlete-at-majority review right" — when Devon turns 18, he gets a one-time formal right to review what footage exists and request specific takedowns (within reason — Groundfloorsports keeps the right to retain footage for archival/historical purposes, but commercial licensing of *his* footage after he's adult requires his ongoing consent, not just the agreement his parents signed when he was 15).

3. "If you film Devon at a tournament, are you filming all his teammates too?"

This intersects with the existing CONTRACTOR-MINOR-FOOTAGE-OVERLAP-RULE.md document. The answer is: yes, incidentally, and the same third-party-minor consent considerations apply. But this isn't really called out in the Track B licensing agreement itself.

**[GAP #4 (Track B): The Track B licensing agreement doesn't reference the third-party minor handling rule. A parent reading just the licensing agreement wouldn't know that other kids in the footage are handled separately.]**

Worth a one-sentence reference: "The handling of other minors visible in this footage is governed by the Contractor / Minor Footage Overlap Rule, available on request."

**Action taken:** I'll assume Tracy accepts the licensing terms after the explanations, but with reservations about the licensing fee. She wants to see the agreement reflect Devon's review-at-18 right before signing. (Reasonable; addressing Gap #3 in real time.)

## Stage 3 — Filming actually happens

**Hypothetical scenario:** Two weeks later, Groundfloorsports' adult contractor (operating under the Videographer Services Agreement) films Devon at a 7v7 tournament. Tracy is present at the event.

**Several systems converge:**

1. The Videographer Agreement governs the contractor's relationship — work-for-hire, footage owned by Subject Medias / Groundfloorsports.
2. The Footage Licensing Agreement governs the Walker family relationship — Devon as primary subject, flat-fee licensing if commercial.
3. The Contractor-Minor-Footage-Overlap rule governs how the contractor handles other kids in frame.

**These three documents are doing different work simultaneously,** and the system handles it correctly. The contractor films the event; Subject Medias / Groundfloorsports owns the footage by work-for-hire; the Walker family has their licensing-fee right reserved; other kids in the footage are handled per the overlap rule (private delivery default, no public posting without separate consent).

This is one of the places where the architecture actually shines. **Three different agreements with three different parties, all consistent, all enforced by the same underlying constraint set.** The earlier work on track separation pays off here.

**Action taken:** Filming happens cleanly. Footage is in Subject Medias' / Groundfloorsports' control.

## Stage 4 — The footage just sits

This is the part nobody talks about.

For Track A, content goes live fast — Marcus posts on his own page, families post their delivered photos, the cycle is days.

For Track B, **the footage just exists**. Devon's footage from the 7v7 event is in storage. Maybe Groundfloorsports posts a wide-action shot on Instagram. Maybe a clip ends up in a "best plays of the weekend" reel. But the commercial-licensing event the contract was actually built around — that's far in the future, if it ever happens at all.

**[GAP #5 (Track B): The Track B operational reality is "the footage sits unused for years." The agreement contemplates a future commercial event; the day-to-day is just storage.]**

This isn't a fixable gap; it's just the nature of the business. But it has real implications:

- **Storage costs accumulate.** Multiplied across hundreds of athletes, this is real money.
- **Discoverability declines.** Footage filmed in 2026 needs to be findable in 2031 if Devon makes the NFL and a brand wants old footage. That's a real archival/cataloging system that needs to exist.
- **The Walker family hears nothing for years.** No content, no update, no fee event. They may forget they signed anything.

**Real recommendation:** an annual "status email" to Track B families confirming the footage is held, summarizing any licensing activity (or none), and reaffirming the agreement is still in effect. Costs basically nothing; maintains the relationship; is a real operational discipline.

## Stage 5 — Five years later, a hypothetical commercial event

**Hypothetical scenario:** Devon is now 21, a college senior, an early-round NFL draft prospect. A brand wants to license his high school 7v7 footage from Groundfloorsports for a commercial.

This is the moment the entire Track B architecture exists for. Let me walk through it:

1. **Groundfloorsports owns the footage.** Per Section 1 of the original licensing agreement signed when Devon was 16. ✓
2. **The brand pays Groundfloorsports the licensing deal value.** This is a regular commercial transaction; Groundfloorsports negotiates the terms. ✓
3. **The Walker family is owed the flat-fee licensing fee.** Per Section 3 of the original agreement. The fee is $500 (or whatever you set in real life). ✓
4. **The Walker family is notified per Section 3 BEFORE the deal closes.** ✓

But also:

5. **Devon's NIL rights as an adult college athlete may apply** independently of the footage licensing agreement. The brand may also need to clear NIL with Devon directly (or with his agent / school NIL collective). That's separate from the Groundfloorsports footage deal.

**[GAP #6 (Track B): The Track B agreement doesn't address the adult-Devon NIL situation. It correctly notes the agreement doesn't transfer NIL rights, but it doesn't address the practical reality that a commercial license of Devon's high school footage probably also requires adult-Devon's separate NIL clearance.]**

This isn't a defect — Section 1 of the template explicitly says the agreement doesn't cover NIL. But it does mean the licensing-fee event is more complicated than the template implies. The brand isn't just paying Groundfloorsports; they're also paying adult-Devon for NIL. Those are two separate transactions.

**Action taken:** I'll assume both happen. Groundfloorsports gets paid by the brand, Walker family gets the $500 flat fee, adult-Devon gets a separate NIL deal with the brand. Everyone is happy. (In real life: probably more complicated; this is a clean version.)

## Stage 6 — A more realistic complication

**Hypothetical alternative:** Devon at 18 (not 21) decides he doesn't want any of his high school footage used commercially. He's not in college yet, not in the NFL pipeline yet — he just doesn't want it. Per the gap I flagged in Stage 2 (Gap #3 — no athlete-at-majority review right in the template), the agreement his parents signed gives Groundfloorsports permanent ownership and licensing rights.

**Legally, the agreement holds.** Tracy and the Walker family signed it; Groundfloorsports has the rights it has.

**Operationally and reputationally, refusing to honor adult-Devon's preference is a problem.** Even if Groundfloorsports is technically right, ignoring a now-adult athlete saying "please don't" is the kind of thing that makes for bad press.

This is exactly the situation Gap #3 was warning about. The template needs an athlete-at-majority review right not because it's legally required but because it's how a trustworthy business should operate. **A right that exists by default in the agreement is a feature; the same right granted reactively under pressure is damage control.**

## Summary — what Track B revealed

| # | Gap | Severity | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No athlete-acquisition workflow / first-contact process for Track B | High at scale | Real operational SOP needed |
| 2 | Licensing fee amount not set; $500 example is probably too low | High | Real business decision before any Track B contract sent |
| 3 | No athlete-at-majority review right in the licensing agreement | Medium-high | Add a review right at age 18 to the template |
| 4 | Track B agreement doesn't reference the third-party minor handling rule | Low | One-sentence cross-reference |
| 5 | No annual status update process for Track B families | Medium | Documented annual communication discipline |
| 6 | Adult-Devon NIL clearance for commercial use isn't addressed in the template | Medium | Cross-reference + a real NIL note in the template |

**What did NOT break:** When the three contracts converged at filming (Stage 3), the architecture worked cleanly — Videographer Agreement + Footage Licensing Agreement + Contractor-Minor-Overlap Rule all consistent, all enforceable. That's the strongest single moment in the Track B walkthrough.

**The biggest finding from Track B:** Track B is *quieter* than Track A. Track A breaks visibly — Mentor pool, signing logistics, post-shoot sorting, all live problems. Track B *seems* fine because the footage just sits there. But Track B's gaps are mostly time-bomb gaps — they don't fire until years later (Gap #3, Gap #5, Gap #6) or until a real commercial transaction happens (Gap #2). The architecture LOOKS solid because nothing is actively testing it. **If you don't address Gaps #2 and #3 before signing real Track B athletes, you're setting up problems that arrive in 3-5 years and arrive at the worst possible moment** (when an athlete is famous and the press cares).
