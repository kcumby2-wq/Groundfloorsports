# Groundfloorsports — Footage Ownership & Commercial Licensing Agreement
**Version:** 1.1
**Status: TEMPLATE — for legal review before any family signs this.** This document is a starting structure, written in plain language, for a real attorney familiar with NIL and minor-contract law to review and finalize before it is used. It is not a substitute for that review. Per state-by-state NIL law, this is not a generic "sign once, covers everything" form — state-specific language may need to be swapped in depending on where the athlete lives.

**What changed in v1.1:** Section 3 now includes an actual proposed fee structure, replacing the "amount not set" placeholder flagged as Gap #2 in the Track B hypothetical run. This is a recommended starting structure for the owner's decision, not a finalized number — see the internal notes for the reasoning behind it and why it's tiered rather than flat.

**Distinct from the CMC/SubjectSkillz delivery model.** This is a different deal than what families get with CMC Visuals-style coverage, where the family owns their footage outright. Here, Groundfloorsports owns the camera and the footage. The family is not getting ownership of the raw film — they're getting a guaranteed, contractual right to a payment if and when that footage is used commercially. That distinction needs to be explained clearly and separately, not blended into the same paperwork as a delivery-and-ownership deal, so a family never confuses one model for the other.

## Section 1 — What Groundfloorsports owns

(unchanged from v1.0)

Groundfloorsports owns the footage it captures of the athlete at events, sessions, and games it films. This includes the raw and edited film, and the right to license that footage for commercial use (advertisements, brand campaigns, broadcast packages, and similar uses) in the future.

This is footage ownership, not a claim on the athlete's name, image, or likeness rights generally. The athlete (through their parent/guardian while a minor) keeps their own NIL rights — Groundfloorsports does not become the athlete's agent, does not control their personal brand deals, and does not get a say in NIL opportunities the athlete pursues separately.

## Section 2 — When commercial licensing actually happens

(unchanged from v1.0)

"Commercial use" means footage being licensed to a third party (a brand, a network, an advertiser) for use in something like a paid advertisement or campaign — not standard recruiting use, not standard social content, not the athlete's own personal use of clips delivered to them under a separate coverage agreement.

## Section 3 — What the family is guaranteed (v1.1 — fee structure added)

**This is the part that has to be a real, written, enforceable term — not a "we'll take care of you" promise.**

- If footage of the athlete is licensed for a commercial use, the family is paid a one-time licensing fee per the tiered schedule below.
- Payment is owed regardless of how well the athlete is performing at the time of licensing, and is not conditioned on continued participation in any program — consistent with state NIL rules (e.g., Texas) that prohibit tying NIL-adjacent compensation to athletic performance or continued play.
- The family receives notice when footage is being considered for commercial licensing, before the deal closes — not after the fact.

**3.1 — Proposed Fee Schedule (recommended starting structure — see internal notes for reasoning; owner should confirm or adjust before this goes into a real contract):**

| Type of commercial use | Flat fee to family |
|---|---|
| Local/regional advertising use (a local business, a regional brand campaign, a local broadcast package) | $750 |
| Use by a brand with a national or multi-state advertising footprint | $2,500 |
| Use that is part of a broader licensing deal where the footage is a featured/centerpiece element (e.g., the athlete is the visible focus of a national campaign, not incidental footage in a broader reel) | $5,000, or 10% of the total licensing deal value paid to Groundfloorsports for that specific use, whichever is greater |
| Any use not clearly described above | Negotiated case-by-case with the family before the deal closes, with a floor of no less than the local/regional tier ($750) |

**3.2 — Fee escalation, not flat-fee-forever.** This is a one-time fee per licensing event, not a royalty — consistent with Section 4's "no resale/royalty structure" rule. But the SAME footage being re-licensed for a second commercial use (a different brand, a different campaign, even years later) triggers a new fee under this same schedule. The family isn't paid once and done if Groundfloorsports re-licenses the same footage again later.

**3.3 — These numbers will likely need revisiting.** Markets for youth athlete footage licensing are not mature or standardized as of this writing; the schedule above is a reasoned starting point (see internal notes), not a number pulled from an established industry rate card. Revisit annually, or any time a real commercial deal happens and the schedule's adequacy gets a real-world test.

## Section 4 — What this is NOT

(unchanged from v1.0)

- This is not a royalty or revenue-share tied to resale, streaming, or any ongoing financial instrument. It is a one-time fee per commercial use (see 3.2 — re-licensing triggers a new fee, but it's still a flat per-event fee, not a percentage stake or resellable asset).
- This is not retroactive — it applies to footage captured after the agreement is signed, not footage already on file under a different arrangement (e.g., already-delivered CMC-style coverage, which the family already owns outright).
- This does not transfer or affect the athlete's separate NIL rights, eligibility status, or ability to sign their own endorsement deals.

## Section 5 — Athlete-at-Majority Review Right (NEW in v1.1)

**This section addresses Gap #3 from the Track B hypothetical run: the original template gave Groundfloorsports permanent ownership with no review point for the athlete once they become an adult.**

**5.1 — At age 18, the athlete (no longer a minor) gains a one-time formal review right** over the footage captured under this Agreement while they were a minor. This means:
- Groundfloorsports will, upon the athlete's written request after their 18th birthday, provide a reasonable summary of what footage exists and any commercial licensing activity to date.
- The athlete may request that specific footage NOT be used in future commercial licensing going forward. Groundfloorsports will honor this for any licensing deal not already contractually committed at the time of the request.
- This review right does NOT retroactively unwind deals already completed before the athlete's request, and does NOT transfer footage ownership to the athlete — Groundfloorsports retains ownership per Section 1. It is a going-forward consent right on future commercial use specifically.
- Groundfloorsports retains the right to keep footage for archival, historical, and non-commercial purposes (e.g., its own portfolio, non-commercial social content under the existing rules) regardless of this review request — the review right is specific to future COMMERCIAL licensing, not all use.

**5.2 — Why this exists even though it's not strictly required by Section 1's ownership grant:** A parent's signature when their child is 15 shouldn't be the only voice that ever gets heard about that child's footage for the rest of their life. Once that child is an adult, they should get a real, if limited, say — particularly because by the time a commercial licensing event is likely (the athlete being recognizable enough for a brand to want their footage), the athlete is probably already an adult. Building this in now, as a standard term, is a feature; granting it only reactively under press pressure later is just damage control.

## Internal notes (not part of the signed page)

1. **Reasoning behind the 3.1 fee schedule.** I researched current market reference points before proposing numbers rather than inventing them blind. A few real data points: a straight licensing fee for footage used commercially has historically landed in the $2,500-$3,000 range in cases where the use was significant. Generic/anonymous stock athlete footage sells for a few dollars because it's replaceable and non-identifying — the opposite of what's happening here, where the footage is of a specific, named minor whose identity IS the value. The tiered structure (local/regional vs national vs centerpiece-of-campaign) reflects that the value of the footage scales with how prominently and broadly the athlete's specific identity is being used — a $500 flat fee (the original placeholder) doesn't track that scaling at all and would look exploitative the moment a real national deal happened.
2. **This is still your decision to finalize, not mine to impose.** The numbers above are a reasoned starting point grounded in available market data, not a settled answer — youth athlete footage licensing isn't a mature, standardized market, so any number involves judgment. Adjust based on your own risk tolerance, what GFS actually expects in deal flow, and legal input.
3. **Keep this separate from the CMC-style consent agreement.** A family should never receive a document that blends "you own your footage" language with "Groundfloorsports owns your footage" language. If an athlete is in both a coverage relationship (CMC-style, family owns output) and a Groundfloorsports-filmed context (this agreement, GFS owns output), they should clearly understand which footage falls under which deal.
4. **This document does not include, and should not be expanded to include:** any tokenization, NFT minting, resale rights, fan/investor ownership stakes, or royalty flows to third parties tied to an athlete's footage or performance. Per earlier discussion in this build, that structure was explicitly ruled out for minors and remains ruled out here.
5. **Needs real legal review before use,** especially Section 5's athlete-at-majority right — that's a meaningful departure from a standard "minor consent transfers to parent permanently" structure and an attorney should confirm it's drafted in a way that's actually enforceable as written, not just well-intentioned.
